15|Core teachings of the book
16|Three phases.
17|Meyer divides every fencing sequence into three: at the onset you attack with guards and cuts (Zufechten), in the middle you press the opponent with handwork (Handarbeit), and at the end you withdraw unharmed (Abzug). He compares them to letters of the alphabet: if you know every letter, you can write whatever the situation demands.
18|Before, After, Simultaneously, Indes.
19|Before (Vor): you attack first and force the opponent merely to respond. After (Nach): the opponent is pressing you. Simultaneously (Gleich): you both cut at the same moment. Indes: seeing the right opening while striking at the left, sensing where the opponent may come from while you move. According to Meyer, Indes is how you get from After back to Before.
20|Parrying (Versetzen) is of two kinds.
21|Merely holding the weapon in the way out of fear, without seeking an advantage, is the first kind; as a habit it is harmful. The better kind is to parry and strike at once. In the old masters' words: “The true fencer does not parry; if the opponent cuts, he cuts too; if he steps, he steps too; if he thrusts, he thrusts too.” Reversed cuts such as the Schielhau, Glitzhau, Kronhau and Zwerch were invented for exactly this.
22|The four-openings drill.
23|Start from the long point. Cut quickly at the upper left, lower right, lower left and upper right openings in turn, each with its step, and withdraw with a Zwerch. Then change the order. Work first with the long edge, then the half edge, then the flat. The diagram of cutting lines shows this sequence.
24|Steps.
25|Every strike has its own step, taken together with the strike. The old masters' twelfth rule: whoever steps after the strike will find little joy in his art. Steps are of three kinds: forward and back, sideways (the triangle step) and broken (concealed) steps.
26|Waiting in the guards.
27|Guards are not for waiting; they name the furthest point you reach when you raise a strike. For example, the highest point you reach when raising an Oberhau is Vom Tag. You pause there for the blink of an eye and, if a better opportunity appears, turn the strike into another cut, such as a Zwerch.
29|Guards (Huten)
30|11 images
31|Meyer counts four main guards (Ochs, Pflug, Tag, Alber) and says the others arise from them: Zornhut, Langort, Wechsel, Nebenhut, Eisenport, Hangetort, Schlüssel and Einhorn. Because the opponent is divided into four quarters, there are four main guards and four openings.
33|Langort — the large figure on the right stands in the long point
34|In the drawing: the large figure on the right stands in the long point
35|How to stand.
36|Left foot forward, arms extended, hold the weapon long in front of your face with the point aimed at the opponent's face.
37|Brechfenster.
38|According to Meyer, the old masters called Langort the Brechfenster (breaking window), because every other play can be broken from it. Langort is “the end of all bindings”.
39|Plays from Langort (p.101–103).
40|Bind with an Oberhau; the moment he raises his sword, cut from below between his arms to his chin. In the bind, break from below with the Rose and throw the short edge to the other side. In the bind, let go of the pommel with your left hand and seize his blade, pass your right hand with the hilt underneath and take his sword by striking up to the right. If he binds with the long edge, go quickly underneath and flick the weak horizontally to his ear.
41|Same figure, another moment.
42|At the end of the Zornhut sequence (p.84), the horizontal strike from the middle line with the reversed flat from the left to the right ear is also shown by “the large figure on the right of plate A”.
44|Ochs — the large figure on the left
45|In the drawing: the large figure on the left
46|Ochs belongs to the upper quarter and comes in two forms, right and left. Right Ochs: left foot forward, hilt high on the right beside your head, point to the opponent's face. Left Ochs: right foot forward, hilt to the left of your head.
47|Ochs in the middle (p.87–90).
48|If you hold your point at the opponent while approaching with a Sturzhau, this is called Ochs; it is an especially good guard for attacking. Attack along any of the four main lines, then cut back along the same line from the opposite direction.
49|Example sequence.
50|As soon as you can reach him, a strong Zornhau to the left ear with a big step of the right foot. When it lands, pull back and cut to his right arm with the long edge; take a wide step to his right with your left foot and pull your head behind your blade. If he extends his sword, let your blade hang behind you, swing the hilt around your head and take his blade strongly away horizontally. Then a Prellhau with the outer flat to his right ear (plate K).
51|If you are pressed.
52|If you cannot take the initiative, let your point shoot into Langort at his face and turn the long edge against the incoming cut; once you catch it, wind on his sword to the nearest opening.
54|Pflug — the large figure on the right
55|In the drawing: the large figure on the right
56|Pflug belongs to the lower quarter; there are two, right and left. Both are really an upward thrust from below. Right Pflug: right foot forward, hilt beside the forward knee, point to the opponent's face, as if you were about to thrust from below. Step forward with the left foot and do the same, and it becomes left Pflug.
57|Hängen from Pflug.
58|If the opponent strikes while you stand in Pflug, raise the hilt and let the blade hang a little toward the ground, catching the strike on your flat. Then wind and work to the nearest opening.
59|The guard cuts pass through.
60|According to Meyer, cuts pass through guards. For example, you start an Unterhau from Ochs and bring it up through Pflug.
62|Vom Tag (Hochhut) — the large figure on the left
64|Left foot forward, hold the sword high above your head with the point straight up. Everything done from above is called “fencing from the Tag”.
65|Why it exists.
66|Tag is the highest point you reach while raising an Oberhau. There you pause for a moment and decide whether to complete the strike or, if a better chance appears, turn it into a Zwerch. It is where the Scheitelhau begins: it starts in Tag, passes through Langort in the middle and ends in Alber.
67|Four plays from Tag (p.75–80), in brief.
68|(1) If he strikes at your left head, spring out of his strike and hit his strong hard with the outer flat so that your blade swings to his head; then to his right arm from below. (2) If he strikes from below to your left, cut with the long edge on top of his strong, and when it clashes, to his left ear with the short edge. (3) If he strikes to your right, step out with the left foot, fall on his strong with the long edge, push the pommel under your arm and strike his head with crossed hands. (4) If he does not strike at all, take the initiative: feint a thrust with crossed hands and work to both sides with Zwerch cuts.
69|How it is broken.
70|If the opponent likes to rise into Tag, go into Schlüssel. Lift your crossed hands above your head, strike with the short edge from below up through his parting line, then throw two Unterhau and a Zwerch.
72|Alber — the large figure on the right, point on the ground
73|In the drawing: the large figure on the right, point on the ground
74|Left foot forward, the point of the sword on the ground in front of your forward foot, short edge up, long edge down.
75|The name.
76|According to Meyer, the name comes from the word for “simple, foolish”. From this guard, once the opponent's strike has been set aside, no full strike can be made without first recovering with a new one. Letting yourself be hit without a ready counter is the fool's measure.
77|Handwork from Alber.
78|Abschneiden: with arms extended or having fallen into Alber, cut away long strikes from both sides with the long edge. Sperren: fall on the blade of someone standing in Alber or Wechsel with your long edge and lock it with crossed hands.
80|Schlüssel (Key) — the large figure on the left, crossed hands in front of the chest
81|In the drawing: the large figure on the left, crossed hands in front of the chest
82|Left foot forward, hold the sword with the hilt and crossed hands in front of your chest. The short edge lies on your left arm, point to the opponent's face.
83|Just as a small key opens a large lock with ease, every other play and guard can be broken from this weak-looking guard.
84|Plays (p.94–95).
85|If the opponent waits in a high or low guard, thrust straight from Schlüssel into Langort at his face. Whichever way he parries, swing your sword around your head along the path he shows you and strike from that same side. If he presses you with strikes, again shoot the long point at his face, turn the long edge against the incoming cut, and once you catch it on the strong, wind inside and outside to his head.
86|What it breaks.
87|You break Vom Tag and Eisenport (Kreuzhut) from Schlüssel: thrust at his face to force him upward, and work from below as he rises.
89|Wechsel — the large figure on the right, point to the side toward the ground
90|In the drawing: the large figure on the right, point to the side toward the ground
91|Right foot forward, the point or weak of the weapon extended toward the ground at your side, short edge turned to the opponent.
92|Meyer's note.
93|Every play done from the other guards can be done even more conveniently from this one; still, he adds a few quick and cunning plays.
94|Play (p.104).
95|If the opponent is in Langort or the straight parry, strike up hard once or twice from right Wechsel with the half edge. On the third, go under his sword, step with the right foot and strike high to his left ear with the flat or short edge (plate F, left small pair). When he lifts his arms straight up, let go with your left hand, turn the blade with one hand, set the point on his chest and take the pommel again (plate F, right small pair). Also used against those who like to run in.
96|Example full sequence (chapter 9).
97|Go into right Wechsel. The moment he raises his sword to strike, take the initiative, go through above and cut a Zwerch from the right; then throw a second Zwerch to the other side.
99|Zornhut — the large figure on the left, sword on the shoulder
100|In the drawing: the large figure on the left, sword on the shoulder
101|Left foot forward, sword on the right shoulder, the blade hanging behind you ready to strike. It takes its name from the wrathful body language.
102|Its relation to Ochs.
103|Every sequence done from Ochs can also be done from Zornhut. The difference is showing a different body language to deceive the opponent.
104|Plays (p.82–86).
105|The moment you can reach him, a quick Zornhau to his left ear, then an Unterhau from the opposite direction to the lower right opening. Before he recovers, fall on his arms, block his movement and make him stagger with an unexpected push. Meyer's rule: “He who cannot cut cannot fence to any purpose.”
106|Verschieben and Winden.
107|Standing in right Zorn, if he strikes from his right to your left, step forward with the right foot and push your sword over your head under his blade. Catch the strike on your flat, thumb underneath. When it clashes, step to his right with your left foot and wind the short edge inside to his head under his sword (plate L, middle small pair).
108|Rule.
109|While in Zorn, if he strikes from below, cut from above with the long edge and the moment it touches, throw your point over his sword to his face.
111|Einhorn (Unicorn) — the large figure on the right, crossed hands raised
112|In the drawing: the large figure on the right, crossed hands raised
113|Left foot forward, spread your elbows to both sides as if going into Schlüssel. Raise your crossed hands up to the right, point aimed outward and up.
114|Entering.
115|Strike up once or twice with the short edge from the right in front of his face; on the third, stay in Langort. Turn the long edge up to the right; the pommel passes under the right arm and the hands cross. Raise the crossed hands.
116|Provoking.
117|With your left foot staying forward, throw two Unterhau and rise into Einhorn again. Lift your left foot slightly and set it down at once. With this body language you provoke him into striking at your left opening.
118|Play (p.91–93).
119|When he strikes, let your blade drop in front of you, pull the sword around your head and cut horizontally against his strike with the long edge; catch it on the strong. Spring to the left, push the pommel under your arm and flick to his head behind his blade with the inner flat or short edge (plate C, left small pair). Then return to Langort (C, right small). Or take his blade from above near the ricasso with crossed hands and pull it to your left (D, right small). Or, as he rises, fall on his arms with the short edge and push with the ricasso (I, right small).
121|Kreuzhut — the middle figure, crossed hands, point on the ground
123|In the drawing: the middle figure, crossed hands, point on the ground
125|Right foot forward, hilt in front of the knee, arms hanging straight down, point reaching up toward the opponent's face; it defends like an iron gate. According to Meyer, this guard was forgotten among the Germans when thrusting fell out of use, but the Italians and others use it. Its main description is in the rapier section.
127|The guard the inexperienced mistake for Eisenport: holding the sword in front of you with crossed hands and the point on the ground.
128|Plays (p.98).
129|If he strikes from above, raise your crossed hands and catch the strike on the strong of your blade. As he pulls his sword back and raises his arms, throw strong Unterhau at his arms; when he comes down, work at his head. Or, the moment the blades clash, wind the short edge to his right ear, wind the pommel back under to the left and cut long to his left with a step back. If he comes from below, fall on his sword with the long edge in Langort.
130|Thrust at his face from Schlüssel, force him upward and work from below as he rises.
132|Hangetort (Hanging point) — the large figure on the right
133|Right foot forward, hold the weapon in front of you with arms extended, the blade hanging a little toward the ground. Meyer notes that the arms in the picture are not straight enough.
134|How it differs from Ochs.
135|It is almost the same as Ochs. But in Ochs the arms are high; here they are extended right in front of your face and the sword hangs toward the ground.
136|Entering (p.96).
137|Strike up hard once or twice from the left toward his face like a wheel. On the third, swing the sword into the correct position in front of your face and return to Hangetort. Repeat until you see your chance.
138|Plays.
139|If he strikes from above, horizontally, from below or at your fingers on the left, step out to his left with your left foot behind your right. Pull the hanging sword to your right shoulder and at the same time strike his head; pull the pommel hard against the inner tendon of your arm so the blade swings strongly. If he comes from above on his right, catch it on the flat and wind the short edge inside to his head, then return to Langort.
141|Cuts (Häue)
142|9 images
143|The four main cuts are Oberhau, Unterhau, Mittelhau (horizontal) and Zornhau; the rest derive from them. The five master cuts are Zorn, Krump, Zwerch, Schieler and Scheitel. Meyer says they are called “master” cuts because every proper play befitting a master arises from them.
145|Unterhau — the small pair at upper left
146|In the drawing: the small pair at upper left
147|How it is done.
148|First cut so that you pass into right Ochs. The moment you can reach the opponent, step and cut diagonally up from below to his left arm; the crossguard ends high above your head.
149|Its place in parrying.
150|Done strongly and thrown with an outward step, the Unterhau takes an Oberhau by lifting it up.
151|Cutting lines.
152|The vertical line can be cut horizontally or diagonally; Meyer shows these lines in detail in the dusack section.
154|Kurzhau — the small pair at upper right, end of the movement
155|In the drawing: the small pair at upper right, end of the movement
156|What is it?
157|A concealed passage.
158|If the opponent strikes from above, act as if you will bind his sword with the short edge in a Krumphau. Instead, go quickly under his sword and strike his head over his right arm with the short edge and crossed arms. This way you have caught his sword with your long edge. At the finish you stand like the small pair at upper right.
159|Meyer's warning.
160|Cuts such as the Kurzhau and Fehlen, which neither parry nor strike, are not really part of fencing. They are used to mislead the opponent, provoke him and draw him out of his advantage; this is often not without risk.
162|Krumphau — the small pair at upper right, a horizontal cut to the hands; also pulling the blade away to the left (Außreißen)
163|In the drawing: the small pair at upper right, a horizontal cut to the hands; also pulling the blade away to the left (Außreißen)
164|Left foot forward, stand in Zornhut. If the opponent strikes, step with your right foot to his left side, outside his strike. With the long edge and crossed hands, cut against his cut or horizontally to his hands between his head and blade; let your blade pass well beyond his arms.
165|From Nebenhut.
166|According to Meyer, the true work from the side guard (Nebenhut) is with the Krumphau: spring to his left out of his strike with your right foot and cut to his head behind his blade with crossed hands.
167|Same drawing, Außreißen.
168|In the Einhorn sequence (p.92): keep your hands crossed, pull your head to the right, throw your blade over his as close to the ricasso as possible and pull his blade to your left.
170|Schielhau — the large figure on the left at the end of the cut
171|In the drawing: the large figure on the left at the end of the cut
172|This too is an Oberhau. It takes its name from being cut with a slight twist, as if squinting.
173|Left foot forward, go into Tag or Zorn. If someone cuts at you, cut against it, but in the strike turn the short edge against his strike and hit evenly with inverted hands. Step well to his left side with your right foot and take your head quickly along with the strike. If done correctly you stand like the large left figure.
174|Counter-winding.
175|If the opponent winds the short edge inside to your left ear from a Zwerch, wind your long edge up against his blade. Then you stand in “the old Schielhau” and strike his head (p.107–108).
177|Zwerch — the small pair at upper left, binding with a counter-cut
179|In the drawing: the small pair at upper left, binding with a counter-cut
180|At the onset go into Zornhut on the right, left foot forward, sword on the right shoulder, as if about to throw a Zornhau. If the opponent cuts from Tag or from above, cut at the same time diagonally from below against his cut with the half edge. Keep the crossguard high above your head so your head is covered. With the cut, step well to his left side; the weapons meet (the two small figures at upper left).
181|Parrying and striking at once.
182|Meyer's example: from Zornhut, when cut at from above, step aside with your right foot and cut a horizontal Zwerch inward with the short edge. You catch his strike on the strong of your blade, right up to the crossguard, while the outer part of your blade hits his ear.

184|Turn to his right and strike his right with the long edge.
186|Windhau — the large figure on the right, long edge over the arm to the head

Pull the pommel back up to your left side with a Rund; the long edge swings over his right arm, behind his blade, to his head (the large figure on the right).

266|Handwork glossary
268|The swords touching each other. One stays in two ways: waiting on the sword to see what the opponent will do, or feigning a withdrawal and coming back to the same place with the short edge. Fühlen is sensing whether he is hard or soft in the bind.
270|A very good handwork. When the opponent strikes too high, too low or too wide, you chase after him into the opening and stop him from finishing his strike. The moment he lifts his sword, you cut beneath it.
272|The best obstacle when pressed with quick sequences. If he stays in the bind and goes around, go to his arm with the long edge, push with the strong or the ricasso and strike the nearest opening.
274|Rising back from a bind on the right and striking or flicking to the other side.
276|In the bind, turning the hand and letting the blade run down with the half edge, while pulling the hilt up for a new strike.
278|Feigning a strike at one opening and hitting another. The Schielhau, Fehlen, Verfliegen, Winden, Zucken and Zirkel are its tools; body language is part of it too.
280|If the opponent wants to meet your strike in the air, you pull the strike to another opening in mid-air without letting it touch his sword. Against those who fence at the sword, not at the body.
282|Not merely parrying a strike crosswise, but at the moment of contact stepping aside and striking an opening at the same time. If it comes from above, rise with the long edge, step to his left with your right foot, and when it clashes, flick the short edge to his head.
284|From Alber, pulling the sword back from your right side and, stepping with the right foot, hurling the strike straight at his head like a stone from a sling.
286|After binding with the long edge, pulling up quickly as if to strike the other side, but striking back with the short edge to where you started.
